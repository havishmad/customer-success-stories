Every print run, matched to the approved shade, before it ships

Towels, bed linen and rugs ship to global retailers who reject shipments when shades drift between print runs. HMC built a colour studio for a home-textiles manufacturer's quality and product development teams: it extracts the palette from any design, converts every colour across HEX, RGB, HSB, HSL, CMYK and CIELAB, scores each run against the approved standard with ΔE2000, groups runs into shade bands and creates new colourways.

The Challenge

Close enough is not a shade match

A print run that looks fine under the shop-floor lights can be visibly different next to last month's run on a retailer's shelf.

Judged by eye

Shade approval relied on experienced eyes under a light box; two checkers could disagree.

Many runs, one standard

The same design is printed across machines, dye lots and weeks; small drifts add up.

Mixed shades in one carton

Without shade banding, slightly different runs were packed together and flagged by the buyer.

Colour numbers in five formats

Designers think in HEX and CMYK, dye labs in LAB, buyers in their own references.

02 · Shade match

Each colour against the approved standard

The approved lab dip for the design is the standard. Every extracted colour is matched to its standard colour and scored with ΔE. Move the tolerance to your buyer's limit; the direction words say how the shade has moved.

Key Takeaways

What any textile maker can borrow

01

Agree the number, not the look

A ΔE tolerance agreed with the buyer ends most shade arguments before they start.

02

Say which way it moved

"Lighter and redder" tells the dye house what to fix; a pass/fail alone does not.

03

Band before you pack

Even passing runs differ slightly; packing by band keeps every carton consistent.
